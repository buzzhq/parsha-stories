## Commentary: the bits worth arguing about

### From laughter to trembling
The Hebrew root צ־ח־ק runs through the whole portion: Sarah laughs (18:12), Isaac's name means "he will laugh," Sarah says everyone who hears will laugh with her (21:6), Yishmael is seen "playing/laughing" (*metzachek*, 21:9) — and then the Akedah, where nobody is laughing. Is laughing at the impossible doubt, or hope? Maybe both. Isaac's whole life starts as a punchline that God insists on telling.

### Hospitality as holiness
Avraham runs to strangers while recovering from surgery. Shabbat 127a: hospitality is greater than receiving the Shechina — he literally pauses a visit from God to greet guests. Sodom is the anti-hospitality city. Rashi on 19:26 (from Bereshit Rabbah 50:4): Lot's wife refused guests salt, so salt became her punishment. Two models of a house: open tent vs locked city. Which one are we building?

### Arguing with God
"Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?" (18:25) is the charter for Jewish chutzpah-toward-heaven. Last week Avraham packed in silence. This week he haggles from fifty down to ten. The secular-friendly angle: justice is a standard even God is held to. Later echoes: Moses, Job, and the Hasidic courtroom stories of Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev.

### The white lie for peace at home
When God reports Sarah's laugh to Avraham, the quote is edited: she said "my lord is old"; God says she said "I am old" (18:12–13). Bava Metzia 87a and Yevamot 65b: great is peace, for even God altered the truth for its sake. A classic *shalom bayit* teaching hiding inside a miracle announcement.

### Don't look back
Lot's wife looks back and becomes salt. Kurt Vonnegut, near the end of chapter 1 of *Slaughterhouse-Five*, says he loves her for looking — "because it was so human" — and calls his own war book a failure "written by a pillar of salt." For immigrant families (Israel → Melbourne, or anywhere → anywhere), the temptation is obvious. What do you carry forward, and what do you have to leave without staring at?

### The Akedah problem
Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (1843) made this the classic philosophy-of-faith text. Many Jewish readers flip the moral: the point is the angel's *don't* — the Torah ending child sacrifice, not endorsing it. Ramban on 22:1: a "trial" lets a person turn potential into action, for the person's sake, not because God needs the information. Pirkei Avot 5:3 says Avraham faced ten trials; the Mishnah doesn't list them, and which list is which is debated — but most traditions put the Akedah last (or nearly last; Rabbenu Yonah makes Sarah's burial the tenth). Leonard Cohen's "Story of Isaac," Dylan's "Highway 61 Revisited," Rembrandt's knife-mid-air: the scene won't leave art alone. Handle gently with kids: it's a story about a sacrifice that is *stopped*.

### Two sons, two near-losses
Yishmael sent into the desert (21); Isaac nearly lost on the mountain (22). Avraham's two near-losses sit back to back. Hagar gets the well; Isaac gets the ram. Both boys are heard. Both survive. The genealogy at the end quietly introduces Rivka — the next chapter of the family already waiting in the wings.

### The haftarah sequel
II Kings 4: Elisha tells the Shunammite woman, "At this season next year, you will be embracing a son" (4:16) — almost word-for-word the angel's promise to Sarah (18:14: *לַמּוֹעֵד … כָּעֵת חַיָּה*). Same vocabulary of impossible births. Sephardim stop at 4:23; Ashkenazim continue through the child's death and revival.

### Did you know? Extra bits
1. **Meat with dairy on the angels' menu** — curds, milk and a calf served together (18:8). Cue the family reaction. (Later rabbis have explanations; keep it a joke at the table.)
2. **Moriah = Temple Mount** — II Chronicles 3:1 says Solomon built the Temple "on Mount Moriah."
3. **The shofar's origin story** — ram caught by its horns (22:13); Gen 21 and 22 are the two Rosh Hashanah Torah readings.
4. **Medieval Jewish comics** — the 14th-c. Spanish Golden Haggadah (British Library) illustrates the three guests and Lot's wife as a strip. Worth pulling up on a phone at the table.
5. **Eshel as hospitality HQ** — Sotah 10a debates orchard vs inn; Rashi there reads *eshel* as *achila / shtiya / levaya* (food, drink, escort). A later variant swaps in *linah* (lodging).

### Pop culture on the sideboard
Wilfred Owen, "The Parable of the Old Man and the Young" (WWI / Anzac angle for Melbourne grandparents): the angel offers the Ram of Pride, "But the old man would not so, but slew his son, / And half the seed of Europe, one by one." Britten set it in the *War Requiem*. Rublev's *Trinity / Hospitality of Abraham* icon is the three visitors as Russia's most famous painting of welcome.

### Questions for the family
1. When should you argue with authority, and when should you just go?
2. Who's the best host you've ever stayed with — and what made them great?
3. Have you ever looked back when you weren't supposed to? What happened?
4. Isaac asked "where is the lamb?" What hard question would you ask on that walk?
5. Optional: Good News Times — everyone shares one piece of good news from the week.
