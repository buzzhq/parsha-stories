## Commentary: the bits worth arguing about

### "Go for yourself" or "go to yourself"?
Rashi (on 12:1) reads *lech lecha* practically: go "for your benefit and for your good." Here you won't have children; there you'll become a great nation. The Zohar (Lech Lecha, R. Elazar) reads the same extra word inward: go "for yourself, to repair yourself, to perfect your level," and elsewhere "to know yourself." Hasidic teachers ran with it, and today it's usually quoted as "go *to* yourself." Two readings, one journey: the trip out is also a trip in. Which one sounds more like the moves you've made?

### Faith without a map
"To the land that I will show you." Avram is 75 and gets no destination. He just goes. Notice, too, that he says nothing at all here. Next week he'll argue with God about Sodom. This week he packs. Is going without knowing brave, or reckless? Is there anything you've started without knowing how it would end?

### Was Avram right in Egypt?
"Say you're my sister." The story makes everyone uncomfortable, and it should. The Ramban (on 12:10) doesn't soften it at all: "Know that Avraham our father sinned a great sin inadvertently by bringing his righteous wife into a stumbling block, because of his fear that they would kill him. He should have trusted in God." He adds that leaving the land because of the famine was also a mistake. Others defend Avram: he was a refugee in a dangerous empire, with no good options. The Torah lets the founder of the Jewish people look scared and flawed in his very first chapter. Why would it do that?

### Strangers and belonging
Avram leaves his country, his birthplace and his father's house. He's a *ger*, a stranger, and his descendants are told they'll be "strangers in a land not theirs" for 400 years (15:13). The Torah keeps coming back to it: you know how a stranger feels. Every family has its own version, whether it's a big move across the world, a smaller one across town, or just being the new kid.

**For the family:** What's our family's story of moving and arriving? Who in the family moved, from where, and what did they bring with them? What made a new place start to feel like home?

### Promises for the grandchildren
God promises Avram the land, and then tells him he'll never really have it in his lifetime: first 400 years of strangers and slavery. Avraham dies owning one burial cave. The stars he counted were for people he'd never meet. What are we doing now whose payoff is for our grandchildren?

### Sarah and Hagar
It's hard to cheer for anyone here. Sarai, hurting from years without children, uses her servant and then treats her harshly. Hagar, used and pregnant, runs. But Hagar gets the most beautiful line in the portion: she names God *El Ro'i*, "the God who sees me." The Torah sees the outsider too. And a lovely coda, many chapters later: when Avraham dies, Yitzchak and Yishmael bury him together (25:9).

### The Abraham Accords
When Israel, the UAE and Bahrain signed their normalisation agreements in 2020, they were called the Abraham Accords, because Avraham is a shared father for Jews, Christians and Muslims. "Father of many nations" (17:5) turned into a diplomatic brand. Is that the promise coming true, or just good marketing? Maybe a little of both.

### "L'chi Lach"
Debbie Friedman's song "L'chi Lach" (written with Savina Teubal) switches the words to the feminine form: *l'chi lach*, "go, you (a woman), to yourself." The call came to Sarai too. She left just as much, and in this portion she gets a new name as well. Who else in the story heard the call without the Torah saying so?

### Questions for the family
1. If you had to leave tomorrow with one bag and no destination, what would you pack?
2. Should Avram have said "she's my sister"? What would you have done?
3. When have you felt like Hagar: unseen, and then suddenly seen?
4. Which name would you change, if you could pick a new one? Why?
