## After the story: learning to see what's in front of you

*This is a family retelling of Genesis 28:10–32:3. The atmosphere, humour and connecting thoughts are storytelling, not extra biblical facts. The squabbling stones, the "Ha-Leah" song, Rachel handing Leah the secret signs, Leah's comeback about Esav, Eliphaz robbing Yaakov, and Leah's prayer for a daughter are all rabbinic midrash and Talmud (mostly via Rashi), not verses. The Torah itself gives Leah no reply on the morning after the wedding.*

### 🍷 Shabbat table question

> Yaakov says, "God was in this place, and I did not know it." **When have you realised — only afterwards — that something important was happening right in front of you (a person, a moment, a place) and you didn't notice at the time?**

Lighter version for Alon and Noam: Laban changed Yaakov's wages ten times. What's the most unfair rule anyone ever made up on the spot to beat you — in a game, at school, or in this family? And for the grandparents: Leah named a son "this time I will say thank you." Who at this table deserves a thank-you they've never quite received?

### Two mornings, one lesson

The story turns on two awakenings that mirror each other. At Bethel, Yaakov wakes up and discovers that God was there all along: *ve'anochi lo yadati*, "and I did not know it" (28:16). In Haran, he wakes up and discovers that the woman beside him is not who he thought: *vayehi vaboker vehinei hi Leah*, "in the morning — behold, it was Leah" (29:25). Both are versions of *I didn't know.* By the end, angels meet him on the road and the Torah says "when he saw them," he knew at once: this is God's camp (32:3).

Rashi's reading of 28:16 is wonderfully human: had I known, I wouldn't have slept in such a holy place. His first reaction to a revelation is embarrassment about the nap. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, in his essay "How the Light Gets In," points out that Yaakov is the patriarch who meets God alone, at night, mid-journey, when he least expects it, and notices the repeated *vehinei* ("and look!") in the dream. His summary: precisely when you feel most alone, God is still with you. (The title nods to Leonard Cohen's "Anthem": "There is a crack in everything / That's how the light gets in.")

A secular-friendly way to say it: growing up is mostly finding out you were wrong about where you were standing.

**For the table:** Is it possible to train yourself to notice things *while* they are happening, rather than in the morning after?

### The deceiver deceived

Last week, the younger son put on a disguise in a dark tent and fooled a blind father. This week, the older daughter wears a veil in a dark tent and fools the husband. Laban's line — "it is not done in our place to give the younger before the firstborn" (29:26) — lands like an invoice. The word he uses for firstborn daughter, *bechirah*, echoes *bechorah*, the birthright Yaakov bought for soup.

The Torah never says "measure for measure." It just lets the words rhyme. The midrash (Bereshit Rabbah 70:19) says it out loud, putting a comeback in Leah's mouth: "Didn't your father call you Esav, and you answered him?" That is midrash, not verse, and it's worth saying so at the table, because the verse is quieter and maybe sharper: Yaakov asks "why did you deceive me?" — *lamah rimitani* — and nobody answers. He is the one man in the story who could answer it himself.

Notice too who makes the switch work in the Talmud's telling (Megillah 13b, quoted by Rashi on 29:25): Rachel. She and Yaakov had agreed on secret signs because she knew her father was a cheat, and on the night she gave them to Leah so her sister would not be humiliated. The romantic heroine is also the one who sacrifices the most.

**For the table:** Is it fair to call what happened to Yaakov "payback"? Or does that let Laban off the hook too easily?

### *Makom*: every place has its rules

The word *makom*, place, runs through this portion like a thread. Three times in one verse when Yaakov stops for the night (28:11). "God is in this place" (28:16). "How awesome is this place" (28:17). Laban's "not done in *our* place" (29:26, *bimkomenu*). The portion ends by naming a place, Machanayim (32:3). Later Hebrew even calls God *HaMakom*, "The Place" — you hear it when comforting mourners: *HaMakom yenachem etchem*.

Rashi (28:11) says the unnamed place was Mount Moriah, where the binding of Yitzchak happened. Rashi also reads *vayifga* as "prayed," and the Talmud (Berakhot 26b) makes Yaakov the founder of the evening prayer: Avraham mornings, Yitzchak afternoons, Yaakov nights. (The same page also gives the competing view, that the prayers correspond to the Temple offerings.)

So the story is about a homeless young man who keeps discovering that every place has its own rules: God's at Bethel, Laban's in Haran. Part of growing up is learning which rules are real.

### Leah, the one nobody noticed

Genesis's most romantic line — seven years "like a few days, because he loved her" (29:20) — sits only a few verses away from its bluntest: "God saw that Leah was unloved" (29:31). Leah's eyes are *rakkot* (29:17). The word is ambiguous (weak? soft? tender?) and the Torah never calls her unattractive. Rashi turns it into a backstory: she cried because the whole town assumed the older daughter would marry the older son, Esav.

Then her children's names become a diary. Reuven, Shimon, Levi: "now my husband will love me," "God heard that I am unloved," "now my husband will be attached to me." And then Yehudah: "This time I will thank God" (29:35). The Talmud (Berakhot 7b) says no one had thanked God since creation until Leah. Rashi (on 29:35) explains her reasoning: I've received more than my share. The person the story overlooks is the one who invents gratitude — and the Jewish people end up named after her son: *Yehudah → Yehudim →* Jews.

**For the table:** Leah stops waiting to be seen and starts seeing what she has. Is that wisdom, or giving up? Can it be both?

### "Am I in God's place?"

Rachel's cry, "Give me children, or I shall die" (30:1), is one of the rawest lines in Genesis, and Yaakov's angry answer, "Am I in God's place?" (30:2), is one of his worst moments. Ramban takes it on directly. He quotes the midrash (Bereshit Rabbah 71:10) in which God rebukes Yaakov: is this how you answer a woman in distress? His own reading is subtler: Rachel spoke from envy and pain, Yaakov refused to play God, and in the end Rachel prayed for herself — "God heard her" (30:22).

It's worth noticing the pattern. Yaakov is brilliant with contracts and stones and sheep. He is much less good with the people in his own tent. Rashi, in contrast, gives the sisters moments of quiet loyalty: Rachel handing over the signs, and Leah praying that her seventh child would be a girl so that Rachel would not have fewer sons than the maids (Rashi on 30:21, from Berakhot 60a).

### The contract man and the boss from hell

Yaakov's vow at Bethel is conditional: *if* God protects me, feeds me, clothes me and brings me home, *then* (28:20–22). He's a contract man. And for twenty years he works for a worse one. Laban changes his wages "ten times" (31:7, 31:41), makes him work seven extra years because of a "local policy," and at the end claims his whole family as company property: "all that you see is mine" (31:43). Think *The Office*, with sheep.

Rabbi Sacks, in "Laban the Aramean," notes the Passover Haggadah's verdict that Laban was worse than Pharaoh ("Laban sought to uproot everything") and admits that, on the grammar of Deuteronomy 26:5, Ibn Ezra disagrees with that reading. Laban never actually harms anyone. The "uprooting," in Sacks's reading, is that possessive grip: the refusal to let his daughters and grandchildren become a family of their own.

Joel Pollak, posting on X in November 2025, summed up the portion as "the great quest; the depths of true love; and the need for boundaries in personal & business life." The boundaries line fits Laban perfectly — and the portion ends with an actual boundary marker, a heap of stones called Mizpah.

Turning point: when Yaakov tells his wives about the spotted sheep, he credits God, not the peeled sticks (31:9). The man who started with "*if* You feed me" ends by saying who fed him.

**For the table:** Was Yaakov's vow at Bethel faith, or a business plan? Is there anything wrong with bargaining?

### Two camps

*Machanayim* means "two camps" (32:3). Rashi says it's two teams of angels: the ones who accompanied Yaakov abroad, and the ones from the Land who came to meet him. That's a deliberate bookend to his comment on the ladder (28:12): angels going up first, then down, as one shift handed over to the next at the border. The story starts and ends with angels at a border, and the difference is entirely in Yaakov's eyes.

Family version: Yaakov is always living in two camps — two wives, two homes, two languages at the heap of stones, and next week, two names.

### A few extras, now that the story has landed

- **The ladder, three ways:** Bereshit Rabbah 68:12 offers several readings. Bar Kappara says the ladder is the ramp of the Temple altar and the angels are priests going up and down. Others say it is Sinai — *sullam* and *Sinai* both add up to 130 in gematria. Rabbi Sacks, in another essay, notes that Jewish prayer itself has a ladder shape: going up, standing in the Presence, coming down.
- **Rashi's geography puzzle (28:17):** the ladder's foot was in Beersheba and its top over Bethel, so its middle was over Jerusalem. Rashi's solution: Mount Moriah "came to meet" Yaakov — the famous *kefitzat haderech*, the shrinking road.
- **Why Yaakov cried at the well (Rashi 29:11):** either he foresaw Rachel would not be buried with him, or because he arrived empty-handed after Eliphaz took everything.
- **The terafim (31:19):** the Torah doesn't say why Rachel stole her father's household gods. Rashi says it was to wean him off idolatry. Scholars have other theories. Let the table guess.
- **Jacob's ladder everywhere:** a rope ladder over a ship's side, the crackling electric arc climbing two wires in every mad-scientist lab, the clacking wooden-block toy, the spiritual "We Are Climbing Jacob's Ladder."
- **The bed trick:** Shakespeare used it in *All's Well That Ends Well* and *Measure for Measure*. Laban got there first, with the whole town in on it.
- **The haftarah (Hosea 12:13):** "Jacob fled to the field of Aram; Israel served for a wife, and for a wife he kept sheep." The prophet sums up this whole portion in one verse.
- **Mizpah jewellery:** Victorian "Mizpah" brooches and split coins carried the KJV wording, "The Lord watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another" (31:49), for lovers who were apart, and they came back during WWI.
