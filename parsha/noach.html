Noach: The Boat That Was a Box

Parashat Noach · Genesis 6:9–11:32 · Shabbat 17 October 2026 (6 Cheshvan 5787)

Here's a fun fact to start with. The most famous boat in the Torah isn't actually called a boat. The Hebrew word is teivah (תֵּבָה), which really means a box or a chest. It has no sail, no steering and no captain's wheel. It's a giant wooden box that floats. The only other teivah in the whole Torah is the little basket baby Moshe floats in on the Nile. So two floating boxes, and in both of them sits the whole future.

And in later Hebrew, teivah means one more thing: a word. Keep that in your pocket. We'll come back to it.

If this were a Hollywood film, you know how it goes: the sky goes dark, the music gets loud, and one ordinary family is chosen to survive while everyone else... doesn't. The Torah got there thousands of years before Hollywood. But the real story is less about the special effects and more about what comes after them.

1. The world goes wrong

Ten generations have passed since Adam and Chava left the Garden of Eden. That's a long time, even when people live for hundreds of years. Here's a surprising detail: Noach was born 126 years after Adam died. So Noach never met the first human, but his father, Lemech, did. Lemech's lifetime overlapped with Adam's. Noach grew up just one handshake away from the man who remembered Eden.

But by Noach's time the memory of Eden had faded. The Torah gives us a strange little scene: "the sons of God" saw that "the daughters of men" were beautiful, and they took whoever they wanted. There were Nephilim in the land too, "the mighty ones, the men of renown." Nobody is quite sure who they were. Giants? Warlords? Celebrities? The Torah doesn't stop to explain.

What it does say plainly is this: "The earth was corrupt before God, and the earth was filled with chamas." Chamas means violence, robbery, lawlessness, the strong grabbing from the weak. Every person, the Torah says, had corrupted their way on the earth. God looked at the world and "regretted" making people at all.

Into that darkness comes one line of light: "Noach found favour (chen) in the eyes of God." Fun bonus: Noach backwards in Hebrew (נח) spells chen (חן).

"Noach was a righteous man, blameless in his generations. Noach walked with God." He had three sons: Shem, Cham and Yefet.

2. The building years

God tells Noach the plan. A flood is coming. Build a teivah.

The instructions are very specific. Use gopher wood (nobody today knows exactly what tree that was). Cover it with pitch inside and out. Make it 300 cubits long, 50 cubits wide and 30 cubits high. A cubit is roughly the length of your forearm, so that's about 150 metres long, longer than a football field and as tall as a four-storey building. Three decks. One window or opening for light near the top. One door in the side.

How long did it take? The Torah doesn't say. Jewish tradition says Noach worked on it for 120 years, so that people would walk past, ask "Oi, Noach, what are you building?" and have time to change their ways. Nobody did.

And here's what Noach does in response to God's plan to wipe out the world: he builds. The Torah says, "Noach did according to everything that God commanded him; so he did." He doesn't argue. He doesn't say a single word. (Hold that thought too. We'll talk about it in the commentary.)

3. Boarding

Then the day comes. God tells Noach: "Come into the teivah, you and all your household."

Now, you probably learned "the animals went in two by two." That's half right. Of every kind of animal there went in a pair, male and female. But of the "clean" animals (the kinds later used for offerings, like sheep and cattle) Noach took seven pairs. And seven pairs of the birds of the sky as well. Noach, his wife, his three sons and their three wives: eight people, plus a floating zoo.

Then comes one of the most tender lines in the story: "And God shut him in." Noach doesn't close the door. God does. Maybe so no one could force their way in. Maybe so Noach didn't have to be the one to shut it on everyone outside.

4. The rain

Noach is 600 years old. In the second month, on the 17th day of the month, it begins. "All the fountains of the great deep burst open, and the windows of the heavens were opened."

This isn't just a lot of rain. Remember the very beginning of the Torah? The world started as tohu vavohu, formless and empty, with water everywhere, and God separated the waters above from the waters below to make room for dry land and life. Now the separation collapses. Water from above, water from below. The world is being rewound to the very first page.

It rains for forty days and forty nights. The water rises above the highest mountains. Everything that breathed on dry land is gone. "Only Noach was left, and those with him in the teivah."

5. The long wait

Then the Torah says something beautiful: "God remembered Noach." A wind passes over the earth, the fountains and windows close, and the water starts to go down.

In the seventh month, the teivah comes to rest on the mountains of Ararat. It takes months more before the tops of other mountains appear.

Noach opens the window and sends out a raven. The raven just flies back and forth, back and forth, "until the waters dried up." Not very helpful.

So he sends a dove. The dove finds nowhere to rest her foot and comes back. Noach reaches out his hand and brings her in.

Seven days later he sends the dove again. This time she comes back in the evening with a fresh olive leaf in her beak. Something is growing again!

Seven more days, and he sends her once more. This time she doesn't come back at all. She's found a home.

Even then Noach waits for God to say the word: "Go out of the teivah." He'd been inside for about a year.

6. A new beginning

The first thing Noach does on dry ground is build an altar and bring an offering. God smells the "pleasing aroma" and makes a promise: "I will never again curse the ground because of people... As long as the earth lasts, seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, will not stop."

God blesses Noach and his sons with the very same words He said to Adam: "Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth." There are some new rules too. People may now eat meat, but not blood. And human life is sacred: "whoever sheds human blood, by a human shall his blood be shed, for in the image of God He made humankind."

Then the sign of the promise. "I have set My bow (keshet) in the cloud." Whenever clouds gather and the rainbow appears, God will remember the covenant: never again a flood to destroy all life.

7. The vineyard

And now the story gets awkward. Noach, "a man of the soil," plants a vineyard. He makes wine. He drinks it. He gets drunk and lies uncovered in his tent.

His son Cham sees him and goes out and tells his brothers. Shem and Yefet take a cloak, walk in backwards so they won't see, and cover their father. When Noach wakes up, he curses Cham's son Canaan and blesses Shem and Yefet. These are the first words Noach ever speaks in the Torah. After 600 years, a flood and a rescued world, his first recorded words are a curse. Hmm.

Noach lives 350 years after the flood and dies at 950.

8. Shrinking lives

Something else changes after the flood. Before it, people lived for 900-plus years. After it, the numbers start falling, generation by generation. Shem lives to 600. His descendants live 400-odd, then 200-odd. By the time we reach Avraham, a long life is 175 years. The world is getting more like ours.

9. The tower

Chapter 10 lists the families of Shem, Cham and Yefet spreading out across the world. Jewish tradition counts them as seventy nations.

But in chapter 11 everyone still speaks one language. People settle in a valley in Shinar and say: "Come, let's make bricks... let's build a city and a tower with its top in the heavens, and make a name for ourselves, so we won't be scattered over the whole earth."

God "comes down" to see the city (a gentle joke: the giant tower is so tiny God has to come down to look at it). God mixes up their language so they can't understand one another, and they scatter across the earth. The city gets the name Bavel, because there God confused (balal) their speech.

10. Ten generations to Avram

The portion ends with a family tree: ten generations from Shem down to Avram, through his father Terach. Terach has three sons: Avram, Nachor and Haran. Haran dies young in Ur of the Chaldeans. Avram marries Sarai, and the Torah notes, quietly, that "Sarai was barren; she had no child."

Terach takes Avram, Sarai and his grandson Lot and sets out for the land of Canaan. But they stop halfway, in Charan, and settle there. Terach dies in Charan.

The flood story began with one family in a box. It ends with another family on the road, about to be told "Lech lecha", go. That's next week.

Commentary: the bits worth arguing about

The disaster-movie problem

Every disaster movie has the survivor who stands on the hill and watches. Noach is the original. Nobody tells us how he felt about it. Did he feel lucky? Guilty? Numb? The Torah doesn't say, which leaves the job to us.

"Righteous... in his generations"

Rashi (on 6:9) notices the extra words "in his generations" and gives two readings. Praise: if Noach was good even surrounded by chamas, imagine how great he'd have been among good people. Put-down: he was only righteous compared to a terrible crowd; next to Avraham he'd hardly register. Rashi says some read it one way and some the other, and leaves both on the table.

Why didn't Noach pray?

When God tells Avraham that Sodom will be destroyed, Avraham argues: "What if there are fifty good people? Forty-five? Ten?" (Genesis 18). When God tells Noach that the whole world will be destroyed, Noach says... nothing. He just builds. His first recorded words come after the flood, and they're a curse. One modern way to say it: Noach preserves, Avraham transforms. Noach "walked with God"; Avraham is told "walk before Me" (Genesis 17:1): out in front, taking responsibility.

Rewinding to page one

The flood waters, from above and below, undo the separation of the second day of creation. The world goes back to tohu vavohu. And then Noach becomes a second Adam: he gets the same blessing ("be fruitful and multiply"), he's a "man of the soil" who plants a garden (a vineyard), he eats the fruit, and he ends up naked and ashamed. The reboot has the same bugs as the original.

Shrinking lifespans

Before the flood, 900+ years. Shem lives 600 (500 after his son Arpachshad was born). Avraham lives 175. Some see a world getting "smaller" and more human; some just see the Torah walking us from legend into history.

The other flood: Gilgamesh

The Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh (Tablet XI) has its own flood hero, Utnapishtim, who builds a boat, saves animals and sends out birds. In 2014, Irving Finkel of the British Museum published the "Ark Tablet" (c. 1750 BCE), in which the Babylonian ark is round, a giant reed-and-rope coracle. A one-third-scale replica built in India leaked. The difference that matters: in the related Babylonian Atrahasis epic the gods send the flood partly because humans are too noisy; in the Torah it's because of chamas, and it ends in a covenant. Borrowed story, shared memory, or deliberate answer? (That's a reading, not a fact.)

Teivah = word

The Baal Shem Tov read "come into the teivah" as "come into the word": step inside the words of prayer and Torah, and they'll carry you through the flood. A box made of words. Which is exactly the Shabbat table question.

Justice and collective punishment

Babies, animals, everyone? It's hard to read the flood as fair, and plenty of Jewish thinkers have struggled with it. The story itself seems to feel it: God "regrets," then promises never again, and hangs up the keshet (a rainbow, but also a warrior's bow) in the clouds. One reading: a weapon put away for good.

Babel: arrogance, or fear of being different?

The classic reading (Rashi): a tower to go to war with heaven. A modern reading: the sin was sameness. Everyone had one language and wanted to stay huddled in one place, "lest we be scattered", when God had said "fill the earth." Diversity is the point, not the punishment. Today we have Douglas Adams' Babel fish and AI translation on our phones. Are we rebuilding the tower, or finally talking across the scatter?
